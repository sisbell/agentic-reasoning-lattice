## Question 9 [theory]

> When content drawn from independently owned sources is gathered into one document, what must remain true about the ownership independence of each contributing source?

## Ownership Independence in Compound Documents

When content from independently owned sources is gathered into one document, **each contributing source retains its complete, independent ownership**. The compound document does not absorb, copy, or take over the included material — it references it. This is the defining feature of transclusion ("windowing"): inclusion by reference, not by copy.

### What must remain true

**1. Each source's owner keeps full ownership and control.**

Inclusion does not transfer ownership. The original lives where it was created, still controlled by its creator:

> "Document A can include Document B, even though Document B is owned by someone else." (2/35)

> "Native bytes of a document are those actually stored under its control and found directly in storage under its control; all other bytes are obtained by front-end or back-end requests to their home locations." (4/11)

Because the bytes are fetched from their home, ownership stays attached to the home document. Only that owner may modify or withdraw the content:

> "Only the owner has a right to withdraw a document or change it." (2/29)

**2. The compound document has its own, separate owner — owning only the new material and arrangement.**

The collage is itself a document with its own owner; it does not become co-owner of the sources it windows:

> "Each compound document is like the other documents: it has an owner and receives royalties." (2/33)

**3. Attribution to each source remains structurally traceable.**

Ownership independence is enforced by the address structure, not by metadata that could be stripped:

> "You always know where you are, and can at once ascertain the home document of any specific word or character." (2/40)

**4. Royalties flow independently to each owner, split by who wrote what.**

Economic independence mirrors ownership independence:

> "If a modified document is read, the original owner and the modifier split the royalty in proportion to who wrote what, as determined automatically." (2/45)

**5. The originals are never damaged by being included.**

> "Thus users may create new published documents out of old ones indefinitely, making whatever changes seem appropriate-- without damaging the originals. This is done by inclusion links." (2/45)

### The underlying principle

This is "reference, not copy." Copying would sever each fragment from its owner; transclusion preserves the independent ownership of every contributing source permanently. As Nelson frames the relationship through the publication contract, inclusion is pre-authorized but never transfers control:

> "permission has already been granted: for part of the publication contract is the provision, 'I agree that anyone may link and window to my document.'" (2/45)

In short: gathering content into one document changes nothing about who owns each piece. Each source remains independently owned, independently controlled, independently attributed, and independently compensated — while the compound document owns only its new material and its arrangement of the windows.
